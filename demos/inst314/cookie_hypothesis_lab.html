The People v. the Cookie Claim

Manager Crumble (a fictional villain) runs the cookie counter at the Y. He swears the average Terp eats only 40 cookies a semester, so he can cut the cookie order and pocket the savings. Students smell a fib. You are the judge. The evidence: one random group of students. The question: is the evidence strong enough to throw out his claim? And because our Terps are simulated, we also have the security tape, so we can check whether each verdict was actually right.

This time · hypothesis test

“Is Crumble’s claim believable, given what we saw?”

Start from the claim. Pretend μ = μ₀ and ask how surprising our x̄ would be. x̄ is now the evidence we weigh.
Is it fair to say the interview “stops being an estimate and becomes an observation”?

Almost. x̄ is the same number either way, and it is always both an estimate of μ and a random observation. What changes is the question we ask of it:

Estimation asks what x̄ tells us about μ, and reports x̄ ± z*·SE.

Testing fixes a hypothesized value μ₀ and treats x̄ as the observed value of a random variable, then asks how unusual it is under H₀: z = (x̄ − μ₀)/SE and its p-value.

Both use the same sampling distribution and the same SE, which is why, for a mean, a two-sided test at level α and a (1 − α) CI always agree (Step 3 shows this). For a proportion the two use different SEs and can disagree in borderline cases (Step 3, the ⚠️ box).

Step 1 · The accusation

Manager Crumble’s claim goes on trial

⚖️

In this courtroom the claim is presumed honest until the evidence proves otherwise, just like “innocent until proven guilty.” We don’t get to convict him because we dislike him; we need evidence that would be hard to explain if he were telling the truth.

So the burden of proof is on the accusation. That’s why there are only two possible verdicts: guilty (the evidence knocks his claim down) or not guilty (it doesn’t). There is no verdict called “proven honest.”

The charge sheet

H₀ is Crumble’s claim (presumed honest). Hₐ is the accusation: what we conclude if the evidence is strong enough.

Statistician’s wording

H₀ (null hypothesis) is assumed true for the purpose of the test; we look for evidence against it. Hₐ (alternative hypothesis) is supported only if the data provide convincing evidence against H₀. The possible decisions are reject H₀ or fail to reject H₀.

Step 2 · The evidence

We question a random group of Terps. How far is their average from his claim?

🔎

We pick students at random and count their cookies. Their average is our evidence. Even if Crumble were honest, a random group would never land exactly on his number: some groups happen to include big eaters, some don’t. So the real question is not “is there a gap?” but “is the gap bigger than luck usually produces?”

To judge that, we measure the gap in standard errors (SE), the typical size of a luck-of-the-draw gap for a group this size. A gap of 1 SE is everyday luck; a gap of 3 SE is a lot to blame on luck. Press “New interview” to question a different group.

      Statistician’s wording

      ⚠️ Proportions: the two viewpoints use different rulers

      📐

      New charge: Crumble claims only 10% of Terps are “big eaters.” We count big eaters in a random group. Now the yardstick depends on the proportion itself: SE = √(p(1 − p)/n). The test stands at his claim and must use his p₀ (that’s what “assume he’s honest” means). The CI has no claim to use, so it plugs in our p̂. Two different p’s, two rulers of different lengths. The gap between p̂ and p₀ is the same from both ends, but it’s measured against different yardsticks.

      Most of the time both still give the same verdict. Only when the evidence sits right near the cutoff can one say guilty while the other says not guilty. Try the presets.

      📋 Rule of thumb: when can the CI stand in for the test?

        One line for students: for a proportion, the CI gives the same verdict as the test unless p₀ sits right at the edge of the interval. If it’s that close, run the test.

        Statistician’s wording

        For H₀: p = p₀, the one-proportion z-test uses SE₀ = √(p₀(1 − p₀)/n) because H₀ specifies p; the Wald CI uses SE = √(p̂(1 − p̂)/n). The two decisions can differ when p̂ is near the rejection boundary; the discrepancy shrinks as n grows. Report the test as the formal decision. The interval that exactly matches the test is the Wilson (score) interval, the set of all p₀ the z-test would not reject; in R, prop.test(x, n, p = p0, correct = FALSE) reports both, consistently.

              Step 4 · Reading the verdict

              Guilty or not guilty: the decision rules, side by side

              🔨

              The bar for “guilty” (α) is set before the trial, so nobody can move it after seeing the evidence. If the evidence clears the bar, the claim is thrown out. If not, the case is dismissed, and Crumble walks out not guilty, which is not the same as proven honest.

              ⚠️
              The CI column agrees exactly only for a mean. For a mean, the test and the CI use the same SE = s/√n, so all three methods always agree. For a proportion, the p-value and critical-value columns still always agree (same test), but the CI column uses SE from p̂ instead of p₀ and can split from them in borderline cases.               Reject H₀ · court: “guilty”

              • p-value ≤ α, or z in the rejection region, or μ₀ outside the CI.
              • Statistician’s wording: “The data provide convincing evidence (at the α level) that the average Terp eats μ₀ cookies.”

              Fail to reject H₀ · court: “not guilty”

              • p-value > α, or z outside the rejection region, or μ₀ inside the CI.
              • Statistician’s wording: “The data do not provide convincing evidence (at the α level) that the average differs from μ₀.”
              • Never say: “we accept H₀”, “the average is μ₀”, “H₀ is true.” Step 5 shows why.
              Step 5 · Why “not guilty” ≠ “innocent”

              “Fail to reject” means “not ruled out,” not “proven true.” The security tape proves it.

              📼

              Real courts never find out whether a “not guilty” defendant actually did it. We can, because we have the security tape: the true average is 42.20 cookies. So we can watch Crumble walk free while lying, over and over, and see exactly why a statistician never says “we accept H₀.”

              ⚠️
              Mean only: here the green band equals the usual CI exactly. For a proportion, the band of claims the test can’t reject is the Wilson (score) interval, which is close to, but not the same as, p̂ ± z*·√(p̂(1 − p̂)/n). The big idea (many claims survive, so “not guilty” ≠ “innocent”) holds either way.

              2An unreliable witness: what if the smuggler lands in our group?

              One student in the population claims 300 cookies. Force him into our sample and watch x̄ jump, s balloon, and the CI stretch until even a wild claim can’t be rejected. The switch changes the interview everywhere on the page (Steps 2–5).

              3What the verdict does and doesn’t say

              “Not guilty” ≠ “innocent”A “not guilty” verdict only means the evidence wasn’t strong enough. Crumble might still be lying, and with a small group of witnesses he often gets away with it.
              “Fail to reject” ≠ “H₀ is true”Our sample couldn’t rule out μ₀, and it couldn’t rule out lots of nearby values either (part 1). An unreliable witness like the smuggler can collapse the case without proving anything.
              Statistician’s wording

              Failing to reject H₀ is not evidence that H₀ is true. It means the data are consistent with H₀ (and with other values in the confidence interval). Conclude: “The data do not provide convincing evidence for Hₐ,” never “we accept H₀.”
